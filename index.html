My name is Javier Ponz. I am in Aberdeen, Scotland. I spent ten years at Apple Retail doing technical diagnosis and customer operations, and I now build and run open agent systems every day. This page is my application, kept public so it can be checked.

The application

I am not going to claim to be a research engineer. What I am is a heavy, technical, daily operator of open agent systems who came from a decade of translating hard technical problems into something an ordinary person can act on — and who can write down clearly what broke and why.

That combination is unglamorous and, I think, undersupplied. Models get better in the lab; they get adopted at the point where a real person hits a real failure and either forms a habit or gives up. I have spent a career at exactly that point, for a company that took it seriously.

I work to one principle, and it has survived every role I have had: leading is serving. It applied when I was alone at a bench with a dead machine, when I was teaching someone else to work that bench, and when I was leading the floor. It applies to software too — a tool earns its place by leaving the person using it with more control than they had before, not less. An agent that makes its owner more dependent has failed at the only thing that mattered.

Forward-deployed work, developer enablement, product feedback, technical support engineering — those are where I would expect to be useful on day one. I would rather be told which of them fits than assert one.

The thesis I am betting on

The interesting frontier right now is not a larger model. It is the operating system becoming malleable enough for an agent to inhabit it.

Two things point the same way. Hermes and MCP make an agent's capabilities into small, inspectable primitives — a tool, a skill, a file, a permission — instead of a sealed product. Omarchy, from the other direction, makes the desktop itself something you alter by editing plain files, and says out loud that it is an OS for the age of agents.

Converge those and you get the thing I actually want: a computer that is proactive and shapeable by its owner, where the agent is not an assistant bolted onto a locked appliance but a first-class citizen of a system whose primitives are open, legible, and yours. Closed, unmodifiable machines cannot get there — the interface has to be editable by the person living in it.

That is the same standard I hold people-work to, aimed at a machine: does the person end up with more agency, or less? Open weights and open primitives are what keep that answer the owner's to give — which is the practical reason I care about the position Nous has taken, beyond agreeing with it.
