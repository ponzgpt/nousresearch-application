APPLICATION

Javier Ponz — ex-Apple Genius, now building agent systems in Aberdeen. For ten years my job was turning complicated technology into something a person could actually use: six and a half years at the Genius Bar diagnosing hardware and software failures, AppleCare calls through the first COVID months, and two stints leading in-store experience across Madrid. I do the same thing now with AI agents.

I am not a research engineer. I run Hermes Agent daily, serve a quantised open-weights model from an RTX 5090 in this room, break things fairly often, and can usually say which assumption failed. Adoption is settled in the half hour after something breaks, when the user decides whether to open it again. That half hour is where I spent a decade.

What I would like to do at Nous is technical support engineering: the work where somebody has to be technical, patient and a competent writer at once, and where a user's broken situation becomes either a fix or a clear account of why it is not one. Ten years of exactly that at Apple is the part of my background that transfers without translation.

Further out, the work I want is forward-deployed engineering. I am not ready for it today and would rather name it than have it inferred. If Nous is somewhere that lets a person carry real support load while learning on the job, that is the path I would like to be on.

One conviction sits under all of it: leading is serving. It meant the same thing at a repair bench, teaching somebody else to work that bench, and running a shop floor — the only honest measure is whether the person you are responsible for leaves with more capacity than they arrived with. Software gets the same test. Does the person end up more capable, or only more reliant.

Why— the problem I would like to be near

An operating system malleable enough for an agent to inhabit strikes me as a more interesting problem than a larger model, and probably a nearer one. Most personal computers are appliances: quick, sealed, arranged according to decisions somebody else made years ago and cannot easily revisit. For a long time that trade was reasonable, since the alternative was a machine you maintained rather than used.

Agents change what the trade costs. An agent's usefulness is bounded by the surface it can reach, and on a sealed appliance that surface is conversation and very little else.

Two efforts seem to me to be converging on this from opposite ends. Hermes and MCP decompose capability into things you can inspect: a skill is a Markdown file, a tool is a server you can read. Omarchy approaches from the system side, treating the desktop as configuration its owner is expected to edit, and describing itself as an OS for the age of agents. Between them sits the machine I would like to use, one that is proactive and shapeable by the person living in it.

I did not arrive here from ideology. I grew up on Windows, because that is what a PC gamer had, then spent a decade on macOS because that was the company I worked for, and used both daily throughout. I am learning Arch and Fedora now, and running Omarchy, and the reason is not novelty. Those closed platforms spent that decade getting steadily worse for the people living inside them, and I would rather own the machine I work on.

You cannot build a malleable machine on a stack you are forbidden to read, and whoever holds the primitives sets the ceiling on how far the agent may help. Open weights and open primitives are what keep that ceiling in the owner's hands, which is the practical reason I care about the position Nous has taken, beyond agreeing with it.

How— what the practice actually looks like

I came to this through OpenClaw. It was the first agent I ran seriously and it did the job I gave it. What changed my mind about the category was finding Hermes — not the model, the harness. Skills are Markdown files I can open and edit. Tools are servers I can read. The loop means the setup gets better through use rather than through me reconfiguring it every week. It took a few weeks of daily use before I was confident that difference mattered as much as I thought, and I have been more right about it than I expected: when I started I was explaining to people what Hermes was, and that is no longer the conversation.

Reading which tool is worth someone's time, early, and being able to say why, is close to the same work as reading what is actually wrong with a machine. Both are mostly about refusing to accept the first plausible story.

Running now, on my own machines and on a VPS I administer:

  • Hermes, daily, as a working tool rather than a demonstration: scheduled workflows, automations, and agents with permission to touch real files.
  • Local inference — llama.cpp serving a quantised open-weights model from an RTX 5090 in this machine, exposed as an API and wired into Hermes. I have also run Ollama and LM Studio. Nothing in that loop leaves the house, which is most of the point of it.
  • A small web application in production, deployed by me with Docker, Traefik and TLS, behind a release gate that refuses to publish when a check comes back red. That one is mine end to end. memento.technoir.cloud

Being exact about the rest of the GitHub account, because you will look and I would rather say it first. The MCP server for Markdown vaults and the thirty-model skill pack were specified by me and written by Hermes from that specification; the design and the contents are mine, the implementation is not, and I could not walk you through either line by line. The Aberdeen newspaper is a fork of an open Hermes project adapted to my city — one commit of seventeen is mine. I keep them public because directing an agent to build something usable is a real skill and the one I am practising, but it is not the same claim as writing it, and the two should not be listed as if they were.

And what I am learning, kept separate on purpose: vLLM, and a homelab I am building on Proxmox — a NAS beneath the hypervisor, the virtualisation and networking that come with it, Pi-hole at the edge. None of it is something I would sell yet. It is the argument in the previous section tested on my own hardware, at my own expense, where a failure costs nobody but me.
