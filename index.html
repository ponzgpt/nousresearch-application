Why— why Nous, and why not the others

An operating system malleable enough for an agent to inhabit strikes me as a more interesting problem than a larger model, and probably a nearer one. Most personal computers are appliances: quick, sealed, arranged according to decisions somebody else made years ago and cannot easily revisit. For a long time that trade was reasonable, since the alternative was a machine you maintained rather than used.

Agents change what the trade costs. An agent's usefulness is bounded by the surface it can reach, and on a sealed appliance that surface is conversation and very little else.

Two efforts seem to me to be converging on this from opposite ends. Hermes and MCP decompose capability into things you can inspect: a skill is a Markdown file, a tool is a server you can read. Omarchy approaches from the system side, treating the desktop as configuration its owner is expected to edit, and describing itself as an OS for the age of agents. Between them sits the machine I would like to use, one that is proactive and shapeable by the person living in it.

I did not arrive here from ideology. I grew up on Windows, because that is what a PC gamer had. Then I spent a decade inside Apple, which is to say a decade of being very good at a platform I did not own. I am now deliberately unwinding both: running Arch, Fedora and Omarchy, moving off Google services, reading up on OSINT to understand what I have been leaking for twenty years, and planning a move to GrapheneOS on my phone. It is slow and occasionally inconvenient and I have no intention of stopping.

I am not doing that because open source is fashionable. I am doing it because I watched those platforms get steadily worse for the people living inside them, from close enough to see the decisions being made. Whoever holds the primitives sets the ceiling on how far the machine may help you, and on a closed stack that ceiling is somebody else's product roadmap. Open weights and open primitives are what keep it in the owner's hands.

That is the practical reason I want to work at Nous rather than at a company that would pay me more to do the opposite. If open inference is going to be a real option for ordinary people and small businesses, somebody has to do the unglamorous work of making it adoptable — answering the questions, writing the fix down, staying with the person until it runs. I would like that to be my job.

How— what the practice actually looks like

I came to this through OpenClaw. It was the first agent I ran seriously and it did the job I gave it. What changed my mind about the category was finding Hermes — not the model, the harness. Skills are Markdown files I can open and edit. Tools are servers I can read. The loop means the setup gets better through use rather than through me reconfiguring it every week. It took a few weeks of daily use before I was confident that difference mattered as much as I thought, and I have been more right about it than I expected: when I started I was explaining to people what Hermes was, and that is no longer the conversation.

Reading which tool is worth someone's time, early, and being able to say why, is close to the same work as reading what is actually wrong with a machine. Both are mostly about refusing to accept the first plausible story.

Running now, on my own machines and on a VPS I administer:

  • Hermes, daily, as a working tool rather than a demonstration: scheduled workflows, automations, and agents with permission to touch real files.
  • Local inference — llama.cpp serving a dense Qwen3 27B, GGUF at Q6, from an RTX 5090 in this machine, exposed as an API and wired into Hermes. I have also run Ollama and LM Studio. Nothing in that loop leaves the house, which is most of the point of it.
  • Omarchy as a daily driver, alongside Arch and Fedora. It is the other half of the argument in the section above, and the first desktop I have used that expects to be edited rather than tolerated.
  • A small web application in production, deployed by me with Docker, Traefik and TLS, behind a release gate that refuses to publish when a check comes back red. That one is mine end to end. memento.technoir.cloud

The hardware half goes back much further than the software. I have been building and tuning PCs since I was a teenager — for games first, then for the pleasure of pulling the last few per cent out of a part through overclocking, which is where I learned that a machine is a negotiation between silicon and the software you ask it to run. The 5090 in this room is the same interest, grown up. It is also why local inference appealed to me before I had any argument for it: I wanted to know what my own hardware could actually do.

The other thing a decade at Apple left me with is a low tolerance for ugly work. Beautiful and useful were never in tension there, and I do not think they are here either — your own branding booklet makes that argument better than I could, and it is one of the reasons this application looks the way it does.

And what I am learning, kept separate on purpose: vLLM, and a homelab I am building on Proxmox — a NAS beneath the hypervisor, the virtualisation and networking that come with it, Pi-hole at the edge. None of it is something I would sell yet. It is the argument in the previous section tested on my own hardware, at my own expense, where a failure costs nobody but me.

Being exact about the GitHub account, because you will look and I would rather say it first. The MCP server for Markdown vaults and the thirty-model skill pack were specified by me and written by Hermes from that specification; the design and the contents are mine, the implementation is not, and I could not walk you through either line by line. The Aberdeen newspaper is a fork of an open Hermes project adapted to my city — one commit of seventeen is mine. I keep them public because directing an agent to build something usable is a real skill and the one I am practising, but it is not the same claim as writing it, and the two should not be listed as if they were.
